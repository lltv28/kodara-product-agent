## 1. The Switch Event

**What was happening right before he reached out.** Bryan's entire income depends on one-on-one clinical work, and the volume is grinding him down. He describes seeing "20-something patients a day" at "three an hour" and calls it "exhausting and dealing with, you know, talking to a depressed, anxious people all day, you know, start to be taxing" (lines 46). On top of the appointments themselves, he is buried in out-of-session admin: "I get a lot of out-of-appointment prescription refills. A lot of random, oh, I need a letter for this, letter for that" (lines 43-44).

**The breaking point / moment of frustration.** The trigger is the explicit realization that he cannot scale himself: "I just had the thought of if I could just teach multiple people at once instead of seeing clients one-on-one" (line 46). The stated goal of the whole switch is "to increase work-life balance" (line 45). A secondary breaking point is the fear of being left behind by AI: "I started thinking about my mom and dad who can't even use an iPhone. And I'm like, do I want to be... left in the dirt, so to speak... just being a relic, just not knowing how to use AI. So I figure let's go all in" (lines 70-72).

**What he tried before and why it fell short.**
- **A self-built course inside Trello, recorded module by module.** This was his original plan and it stalled hard: "I'm going to basically have my course inside of Trello, and I'm going to videotape... It's been a nightmare. I'm going to video each module, and I'm like, that's going to take the rest of this year to do that" (lines 61-63). The DIY course path was too slow and too labor-intensive to ever finish.
- **Other AI vendors he saw advertised.** He shopped around: "there's been a couple, I think Eugentic AI, there's a few others" (line 64). He had seen Kodara's ads "probably for months now" before clicking (line 65).
- **Co-Pilot for letter-writing.** He already uses generic AI in a piecemeal way: "I use Co-Pilot or whatever to write it" for disability letters (line 408), but it is not connected to patient history or automated.
- **GoHighLevel (high level) CRM** that he bought but never operationalized: "I currently have high level, which I'm not really using, but I anticipate using it" (line 462).

## 3. The Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

**Functional job.** Turn his clinical expertise into an AI product that (a) teaches other providers and patients what a psychiatrist knows, (b) offloads repetitive admin (refill orders, disability/citizenship/accommodation letters, appointment and prescription reminders, prior-auth friction), and (c) delivers a paid, scalable ADHD assessment with a diagnostic-interpretation report. In his words: "have it assist with ADHD assessments... there's a link that you can send to the individual, they take the test... they pay the fee... for a higher fee, we can write you a diagnostic interpretation" (lines 489-495). Also surfaced: certification exam test prep and a medication mechanism-of-action teaching tool (lines 499-500).

**Emotional job.** He wants relief from being the sole bottleneck and from carrying the emotional load. The driver is work-life balance and not feeling "taxing[ed]" by an unending stream of depressed, anxious patients (lines 45-46). He also wants to feel current and relevant rather than obsolete: "I don't want to be left in the dirt... just being a relic" (line 71). And he wants the calm of nothing falling through the cracks (the Amazon-tracking metaphor he agreed with, lines 366-370).

**Social job.** He wants to be perceived as the authority whose brain other providers want in their pocket: "psychologists and therapists really have a craving to learn about psychiatric medications" (line 55), and his course promise "know what your psychiatrist knows" (line 505). He frames himself with earned-expert language: "I think I'm a suicide expert... I'm a child expert" (lines 282-283). Lucas mirrored this status framing ("I feel like I'm meeting a celebrity," line 612). He also enjoys the position of being the rare provider who genuinely listens: "I've had many patients tell me, you listen to me... they feel heard" (lines 106-108).

**Cognitive dissonance / stated vs. underlying driver.** His stated reason for the switch is altruistic and educational ("teach multiple people at once," "increase work-life balance"). But the underlying energy on the call clusters around revenue and status: the ADHD assessment as "a high ticket" (line 253), wanting "to exploit that" demand from therapists "which probably would be to the detriment of many psychiatrists" (lines 504), and repeatedly returning to monetizable reports. There is also a tension between his attachment to deep one-on-one relationships ("very difficult to let them go," line 117) and his goal of removing himself from one-on-one delivery. The relationship/rapport is the very thing he says makes him effective ("once they trust you... whatever medications you give them, they will come back and say, yeah, it's helping," line 114), yet the product asks an AI to stand in for that rapport. That gap is the core risk to his belief in the product.

## 6. Stall Risk Score

**Score: 3 / 5 (moderate).**

Bryan is genuinely motivated, flexible, easy to work with ("I'm pretty easy to work with," line 593), and self-identified clear monetizable use cases, which pulls risk down. But the build is bottlenecked on a chronically overloaded clinician supplying "thousands and thousands of notes," HIPAA remains an open concern he raised himself, and there is no signed contract or committed launch date yet, which pulls risk back up to the middle.

**Recommended intervention:** Immediately after the call, get one small, fully de-identified content sample into the new Google Drive (one ADHD-assessment workflow or a handful of scrubbed notes) and send a one-page written HIPAA/data-scrubbing explainer plus contract confirmation in the same Slack message. Landing a tiny first upload while motivation is high de-risks the data dependency, and closing HIPAA in writing removes his stated blocker.
