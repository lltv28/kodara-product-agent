## 1. The Switch Event

**What was happening in the business right before she reached out.**
Sue already runs a fully built, largely automated mortgage brokerage and is now deliberately starting a *second* business — AI/process consulting for small business owners. She is in the very early build-out stage of that consulting business: "No, it's so new right now that I haven't gotten to that stage yet" (line 94), and "this was like in creation in my mind when I came across the post and I booked with Nora" (line 99).

**The trigger / decision point.** The switch is a deliberate, self-initiated strategic pivot rather than a crisis. She put a stake in the ground this year: "this year I really kind of put pen to paper and I'm like, I'm going to go full blown with this business and really hit the ground running because I feel like it is the next big wave and there's such a huge demand for it" (line 44). She found Kodara through a social post and booked a call: "when I came across the post and I booked with Nora" (line 99).

**The breaking point / source of frustration.** Not personal burnout in the present (the mortgage business is "self-sufficient," line 33), but a generalized frustration watching other business owners waste time: "it boggles my mind today that there's still people sitting for, you know, working 12 hour days and bogged down by their email" (line 46) and "I had a meeting with someone last week and she's not using a booking link. She's spending like hours going back and forth with people" (lines 49-50). Her own past is the deeper push: "having gone through so many years of scaling and building a business and dealing with working crazy hours and burnout" (line 45).

**What she had tried before, and why it fell short.**
- She is nearly finished a formal consulting course on process implementation/strategy and assessments: "I took a full consulting course just on like process implementation and strategy and doing assessments for businesses" (line 54). This gave her tools (custom GPTs, an AI readiness assessment) but not a productized, marketable low-ticket entry offer.
- She chairs a paid peer group via "Tech Canada... which is part of Vistage" (line 95) and gets paid for it, "but that's not enough, like there's so much more I want to do" (line 97).
- She has done one-off, in-person consulting (the landscaper) the manual way, which she explicitly does not want to repeat: "we had a meeting like in person and I was mapping it out. Not my preference. Don't want to meet people in person" (lines 154-156).

The gap Kodara fills: she has expertise, credentials, and even assessment assets, but no scalable funnel, no low-ticket front-end offer, and no marketing engine. "I understand that there's better and faster ways to do it and pay for it. And this is why we're here today" (lines 325-326).

## 3. Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

**Functional job.** Get a productized, scalable, automated low-ticket front-end offer (an AI-powered business/process assessment) plus the marketing engine and AI "brain" to sell it, so leads flow into a mid- and high-ticket consulting ladder without her doing manual, in-person, one-off work. "what could we do as a low ticket item to bring them in? That's something that... we figure out" (lines 104-105); the AI should run the assessment automatically and then "have the option to book a consult with me and move forward to maybe the high ticket or the medium or high ticket items" (line 408).

**Emotional job.** To feel challenged and energized by a new mountain after conquering the first, and to feel that progress is fast. "I built one successful business. Great. Good. It's there going. And then now I need the next challenge" (lines 58-62); "one of those crazy people... has to be challenged all the time" (lines 62-63); "I'm excited. Obviously, you can like hear that I'm excited about it" (lines 75-76); relief that she can offload the build: "I feel like this is going to just propel what I've been wanting to do... expedite it and get it out to the public faster" (lines 580-581). She also wants to feel in control of pace and quality (review-and-approve, don't get overwhelmed).

**Social job.** To be seen as the modern, forward-thinking authority who helps "old school" business owners adopt AI the smart way, in contrast to her father's generation. "I grew up in a family business. My father was really successful, but definitely did things the old school way... there was that family struggle where he's still seeing his ways. So I just, I want that opportunity to put it out there for these businesses to find a smarter and better way to work" (lines 65-70). She also wants to be perceived as un-fearful and ahead of the adoption curve: on AI-generated posts, "I'm like, actually it's not [the worst thing]. What are you afraid of... It doesn't bother me whatsoever" (lines 331-335).

**Cognitive dissonance flag.** Her stated reason is altruistic and market-timing ("huge demand," "next big wave," helping overwhelmed owners). The underlying driver is more personal: identity as a serial builder who needs a new challenge ("now I need the next challenge," "has to be challenged all the time") and a desire to do what her father wouldn't ("find a smarter and better way to work"). This matters for messaging — she will respond to being positioned as the visionary/builder, not just as a helpful service provider.

A second, smaller dissonance: she "loves marketing" and would build it herself, yet is hiring it out for speed. She is delegating something she's emotionally attached to, so she will want visibility and approval rights early ("usually for the first few batches, we always send it to you. Make sure you review it," lines 479-480, which she accepts readily).

## 6. Stall Risk Score

**Score: 2 / 5 (low-to-moderate; likely to launch reasonably fast).**

Justification: Sue is highly motivated, decisive, AI-friendly, type-A about speed, and already owns the core IP (course assessments and custom GPTs) that drastically shortens the AI build, which all point to a fast launch. The pull toward stall comes from a not-yet-defined low-ticket offer/price, marketing channels (Facebook/Instagram) that don't exist yet for the new brand, and live process gaps (the spam'd email and the missing contract) that could slow the first 48 hours.

Recommended intervention: In the first preview cycle, lock the offer and price *and* ship a working, fully-automatic assessment demo built directly from her existing course GPTs, so she sees a fast, hands-off quick-win that resolves her "$27 but slow to execute" anxiety before momentum cools — and resend the contract plus fix email deliverability immediately.
