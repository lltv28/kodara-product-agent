# Milton Brown — Onboarding Call JTBD Analysis (2026-05-11)

Client: Milton Brown, financial advisor (Terian Consulting FS), 23 years in financial services, sells fixed indexed annuities to pre-retirees and retirees aged 50 to 65.

---

## 1. The Switch Event

**What was happening in their business right before they reached out.** Milton had returned to the industry full-time after a roughly 10-year joint-venture practice that he sold, and he found the business had changed beneath him. In his words: "when I came back, it was like, you know, going from the dark ages into the information age, right? It was just like, no longer do you do financial services the way that I knew. You know, you make a list of your top 100 people, and you don't want to do that. So I realized that I had to start the business all over again, basically." He had been cycling through prospecting methods: "I was trying seminars, educational workshops, buying leads, all these various things."

**The breaking point / moment of frustration.** The most recent and most acute trigger was a failed engagement with a marketing vendor, Wojo Media. "To say I was disappointed would be an understatement. It did nothing to, you know, that it said it would do." The specific betrayal was the vendor taking four months and significant ad spend before diagnosing the obvious: "after 40 months [sic: four months] of paying for ad spend, of course, already paying their fee on the front. Well, we realized that the issue with your demographic, which is pre-retirees and retirees between the ages of 50 to 65, the challenge is trust and credibility." Milton's reaction: "That makes sense to me, but it took you four months to figure that out when you're advertising to financial advisors... You came to me saying, hey, I know exactly what you need to do... And now four months later, you come and tell me, oh, I figured it out."

**What they had tried before and why it fell short.**
- **Wojo Media (paid ads program):** Failed to deliver; charged a front fee plus months of ad spend before identifying the core trust problem. "It did nothing to... do."
- **FMO social security workshops (the thing that partially worked):** "I got with an FMO who does social security workshops, and they give you the whole presentation, the spiel, the PowerPoint... I've done that, and I've had some success with that." The shortcoming was both cost/dependency on a mailing system and an ethical discomfort: "I wouldn't say it's a bait and switch, but I also wouldn't say it was completely above board... I'm using the Social Security as a carrot when I'm really just trying to find out where your assets are."
- **Seminars, educational workshops, buying leads:** Tried, none singled out as durable wins.

**What pulled him toward Kodara specifically.** The novelty of the AI angle layered onto a low-ticket model he already understood conceptually: "Now, of course, I've heard of low ticket, medium ticket, high ticket... But when I saw your spin to it of using AI, I was like, oh, my God. So that's how I arrived here."

## 3. The Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

**Functional job:** Generate qualified pre-retiree/retiree leads who already understand and believe in the annuity solution, so that by the time they reach a consultation they are pre-sold and ready to buy his high-ticket offer. He frames his real product plainly: "at the end of the day, when I get through the whole show, I'm selling annuities... my bread and butter, my high ticket offer is an annuity." He wants the AI to do the belief-building groundwork (Lucas: "by the time that they're having that higher-level sales conversation, they already believe what you're telling them" — Milton: "Yeah, that definitely makes sense").

**Emotional job:** To feel he is no longer being taken advantage of by marketing vendors, and to operate in a way that feels honest. The Wojo wound is about being misled by someone who claimed expertise; the workshop discomfort ("not completely above board") signals he wants a method he can feel clean about. He wants relief from the "start the business all over again" overwhelm and confidence that he is finally on a modern, working system.

**Social job:** To be perceived as a credible, trustworthy advisor by a skeptical 50-to-65 demographic where, by the vendor's own diagnosis, "the challenge is trust and credibility." He also wants to be seen as straight-shooting rather than as the typical advisor who hides the annuity sale: he admires the competitor who is plainly "the annuity company" and is openly weighing whether to be that direct versus the industry norm of "we try to shroud it underneath this whole, I do holistic planning."

**Cognitive dissonance / stated vs. underlying driver.** Milton's stated reason for being here is the shiny new mechanism: "when I saw your spin to it of using AI, I was like, oh, my God." But his underlying driver is the trust-and-credibility gap with his audience plus a need to stop being burned by marketers. The AI is the hook; trust restoration and reliable lead flow are the real jobs. A second tension: he wants to be honest and direct ("the annuity company" model appeals to him) yet his proven, partially-working method relied on a Social Security "carrot" he himself flags as "not completely above board." He is asking Lucas to resolve that conflict for him: "as a marketer, what approach do you think is the best? Just say straight in the door, this is who I am... Or... I'm a wealth advisor... and eventually lead them to where you want to go."

## 4. Expected Outcomes & Success Criteria

**What he pictured when signing up.** A modern, AI-powered version of the low-ticket-to-high-ticket model that finally fixes the trust problem his last vendor only named after the fact. He still could not fully visualize the product, and said so: "I was trying to think about, you know, what an AI tool for this space would look like. And I just was stopped." This is a signal that his success picture is not yet concrete and is being shaped live by Lucas on the call.

**What result would make him call this a win.** Implicitly: paying clients who ascend to annuity purchases (his $250k-minimum, high-commission core offer), generated through a system that does not require four months of dead ad spend. He cares about the post-launch reality, asking directly about "the ramp-up... in terms of not only just the perfect purchasing of the AI tool, but also, you know, bringing clients aboard." A win is buyers who actually ascend, not just low-ticket nibblers (a risk Lucas himself names: "people are not ascending... they're buying the first thing, but they're maybe not the right fit").

**Timeline expectation vs. actual delivery.** Milton anchored hard on the timeline and double-checked it twice: "if we meet the standard of two to two and a half weeks, then we're looking at a launch around mid-June, right?" Lucas confirmed: version 1.0 in roughly 14 to 18 days, worst case 30 days, "probably around like June 11th... second week of June," with an early preview "by Wednesday or Thursday." Milton's expectation and Lucas's stated delivery are aligned, but Milton is clearly tracking the date and will measure against it.

## 5. Product Insights for PM

**Feature gaps or requests implied but not directly stated.**
- A goal-driven retirement projection / calculator that works backward from the client's dream scenario to the required "money math" and visualizes safety-floor outcomes. Lucas floated it and Milton affirmed ("Yes"): "something like a calculator... the first time I saw, like, a compound interest graph... the money just grows." This is the most concrete AI-product feature surfaced. (Supporting: Milton's own framing that "Retirement is more than just a single number" and that people both over- and under-estimate what they need.)
- An annuity-type explainer/decision aid that maps client goals (income vs. legacy vs. certainty) to the right product type (fixed, variable, fixed-indexed) and bonus structures — Milton spontaneously taught the full three-type taxonomy, which is ready-made AI brain content.
- AI avatar / clone video for front-facing ads and a 5-to-10 minute pre-purchase VSL (raised by both parties; Milton flagged wanting "one that looks like me, but that's not me").

**Onboarding friction points.**
- Asset-collection friction: "I know I was supposed to create a Google Drive with some links... and I couldn't figure that out." He instead has a Word doc with articles, a Social Security webinar (~30-min snippet), 3 to 4 YouTube videos, a podcast, PR articles, and social profiles. PM should make asset intake idiot-proof (a link Lucas offered to send) rather than relying on the client to build a Drive.
- Conceptual fog about the product: "I was trying to think about... what an AI tool for this space would look like. And I just was stopped." He needs the very early preview to make the deliverable real for him.

**Trust and confidence signals relevant to Trust Report / Brain Health dashboard.**
- His entire market's gating factor is trust: vendor diagnosis quoted as "the challenge is trust and credibility." Any Trust Report should foreground how the AI brain establishes advisor credibility to a 50 to 65 audience.
- AI-clone authenticity is a confidence concern ("looks like me, but that's not me"). Brain Health / quality signals showing the avatar is convincing will reassure him.
- He has been burned before and is watching for proof, not promises (the Wojo four-month grievance). Early-data milestones and a no-dead-spend guardrail are trust currency here; Lucas leaned on exactly this: "I would definitely not advise three or four months of ad spend with no results."

**Stall risks.**
- Single-source asset dependency: the whole AI build is gated on one Word document he must hand over; he already showed friction with file logistics.
- Ascension risk: his real revenue is the annuity, not the $17 product. If buyers do not ascend, he will judge the engagement a failure quickly given his prior history. Lucas pre-named this scenario.
- Unresolved positioning decision: he explicitly asked Lucas to choose between "straight in the door, this is who I am" (annuity) vs. "I'm a wealth advisor... eventually lead them." If this is left ambiguous, marketing direction could stall.

**Messaging / positioning language the client used that we could mirror in marketing.**
- "Personal pension" / "private pension" — his industry's softening phrase for an annuity: "When you hear someone says, I help people create personal pensions" / "I can help you create a private pension, a personal pension."
- "Social security is an annuity" — his single best plain-language explainer; powerful because every working American understands it.
- "Recession-proof" / can't-lose framing: "You're guaranteed not to lose a dime. The only thing you can do is make money or keep what you got." Lucas echoed "recession-proof."
- "Take a portion, not all... make it safe... put it on the sideline" — his core, non-pushy pitch logic.
- "All the up potential of the market, none of the downside" — clean FIA hook.
- The bull-market objection-handling insight: "this bull run has created a false sense of... optimism," and the need to validate, not doom ("we don't want to come in with like a doom"). Marketing should piggyback existing optimism, not fight it.
