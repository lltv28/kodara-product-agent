## 1. The Switch Event

**What was happening in the business right before he reached out.**
Ray had a profitable drywall/painting business and a long-standing ambition to franchise it, but his attempt to formalize that ambition stalled. He had become a legal Canadian franchise and was "ready to just kind of hit the ground running" (line 10), then concluded franchising was the wrong vehicle: "after, you know, reaching out, talking to a lot of people, I kind of felt like I was in the wrong lane a little bit, and that maybe franchising wasn't for me" (line 11).

**The breaking point / moment of frustration.**
The trigger was the friction of re-engaging the legal franchise machinery. As he tried to restart it: "as I'm reaching out to the lawyers and trying to, you know, put the steps in motion again, I'm realizing, ah, this is, you know, complicated. This is why I didn't want to do this in the first place. It's, you know, it's really a tricky battle" (lines 15-17). He pivoted the concept to brand licensing: "what I circled around to was, you know, it's more of like a brand licensing thing" (line 18).

A second, more positive trigger: early proof that the licensing idea sells, paired with the realization he cannot convert demand on his own. "I talked to one, like 50 different guys already, and I sold one, like my first one last week. I got a $1,000 subscription. That's my first subscription I've ever sold" (lines 36-38). But: "there's just a ton of interest. ... It's just that I don't know how to really nurture the interest and turn it into practical leads, really" (lines 39-41).

**What he had tried before and why it fell short.**
- **Legal franchising:** became a legal Canadian franchise, then abandoned it as too much control and a poor fit. "it seems like you're a lot of control. Just the type of business that I'm running, it's not a fit for investors, more of a fit for operators" (lines 12-13). Also: the only buyers of a franchise fee lack the skill. "the only people with who are going to spend money on a franchise fee don't typically have the skills" (line 103).
- **Manual outbound selling:** talked to ~50 prospects and closed only one. "I talked to one, like 50 different guys already, and I sold one" (line 36).
- **Organic Facebook posts to poke pain points:** generate attention but not conversion. "When I put out these random Facebook posts trying to like poke pain points and stuff, I get some fire started, and I can see that there's a huge interest" (line 40), but "I don't know how to really nurture the interest and turn it into practical leads" (line 41).
- **ChatGPT for SOPs / thinking:** already a heavy user. "you can log into my chat GPT account. ... I'm always talking to that and making SOPs and messing around" (lines 441-442). It helps him think but has not produced a sellable, scalable system.

## 3. The Functional, Emotional, and Social Jobs

**Functional job (the practical task).**
Turn his proven Drywall Medic playbook into a productized, automated brand-licensing offer with a clear low-ticket entry point, and convert the interest his posts already generate into paying licensees without him selling each one by hand. Stated directly: "All I know is that I'm attracted to the sales funnel" (line 35) and "I don't know how to really nurture the interest and turn it into practical leads" (line 41), plus the unresolved offer question "I don't know what a low-ticket item really looks like for this" (line 34).

**Emotional job (how he wants to feel).**
He wants to feel in control and capable without being trapped by complexity or by an identity he rejects. The relief he's seeking is escaping the "tricky battle" of franchising (line 17) and the "pressure to do a lot of consulting" (line 180). He wants the validation that his idea works at scale, building on the spark of his first sale: "That's my first subscription I've ever sold. ... I'm pretty stoked about that" (lines 38-39). Curiosity and hope are present: "I'm really curious to see what you guys have to build" (line 416) and "Excited" (line 575).

**Social job (how he wants to be perceived).**
He wants to be seen as the operator/brand behind a polished, trustworthy home-service network — the "Uber for drywall repair" (line 202) — not as a guru or coach. The desired market perception is captured in his positioning language: "we present ourselves as like the clean cut, polished, super ... we're going to protect the heck out of your house" (line 357). For his own status he wants to be the system owner ("Drywall Medic, California, operated by Lucas Contracting," line 203), explicitly distancing himself from the coach/consultant persona: "I'm not a coach" (line 179).

**Cognitive dissonance flag.**
Ray's stated reason ("I'm not a coach," line 179; "We have nothing to do with their success," line 388) conflicts with the underlying driver of his own offer. The thing he's actually selling — "You get access to some of our sales systems, how we do our advertising, how we attract homeowners, how we present ourselves" (line 183) — is education and consulting in product form. Lucas names this gap directly: "you don't want to be a coach or a consultant, but that's a lot of the things that AI can do because a really good consultative sales process is going to have a little bit of that belief shifting" (line 254). A second dissonance: he insists "We have nothing to do with their success" (line 388) yet is gating the brand on quality ("you get three bad Google reviews, then you lose it," line 409) and worrying aloud about protecting reputation (line 372) — he is more invested in licensee outcomes than he wants to admit. The AI offer resolves the first dissonance by letting the system coach so Ray does not have to.
