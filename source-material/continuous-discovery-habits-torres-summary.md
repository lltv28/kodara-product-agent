# Continuous Discovery Habits by Teresa Torres (2021) - Comprehensive Summary

## About the Book and Author

Teresa Torres spent 14 years in product leadership roles before burning out on the daily work of evangelizing product management at founder-led startups. In 2013, she left her VP of Product and Design role to become a product-discovery coach. Over seven years of coaching hundreds of product trios through a 12-week program, she iterated on her curriculum the same way a product team iterates on a product: tracking cohorts, measuring where teams got stuck, and refining her methods. This book is the distilled output of that work.

The book's central thesis: product teams should make product decisions continuously, not in big upfront planning cycles, and those decisions should be infused with direct customer input at least weekly. Discovery is not a phase that happens before development. It is a parallel, ongoing activity that never stops.

Torres's formal definition of continuous discovery:

- At a minimum, weekly touchpoints with customers
- By the team building the product
- Where they conduct small research activities
- In pursuit of a desired outcome

## Part 1: What Is Continuous Discovery?

### Chapter 1: The What and Why of Continuous Discovery

Torres traces the evolution of product work through three eras:

1. Traditional (pre-2001): Business leaders owned discovery. Annual budgeting cycles. Fixed projects with fixed timelines. Product shipped, customers didn't want it, waste accumulated.

2. Post-Agile (2001-2015): Shorter delivery cycles, more customer feedback, rise of UX research. But leaders still clung to ownership of discovery. Usability testing happened too late. Teams were still measured by output, not outcomes. Discovery and delivery remained separate responsibilities.

3. Continuous (present): Teams engage with customers regularly, test assumptions, co-create with customers, and adapt in real time. Discovery and delivery happen in parallel.

Six prerequisite mindsets for continuous discovery:

1. Outcome-oriented: Define success by value created, not code shipped
2. Customer-centric: Elevate customer needs to be on par with business needs
3. Collaborative: Reject siloed handoffs, embrace cross-functional team decisions
4. Visual: Externalize thinking through maps, trees, and drawings
5. Experimental: Think like scientists identifying assumptions and gathering evidence
6. Continuous: Evolve from a project mindset to a continuous mindset

The book is written for the "product trio" (product manager, designer, software engineer) working together. Torres acknowledges most teams need more than three people, but the trio is the minimum decision-making unit that should adopt these habits together.

### Chapter 2: A Common Framework for Continuous Discovery

Torres introduces the Opportunity Solution Tree (OST) as the central visual framework. The tree has four levels:

1. Desired outcome (the root): The business need the team is pursuing
2. Opportunity space: Customer needs, pain points, and desires that could drive the outcome
3. Solution space: Ideas that might address those opportunities
4. Assumption tests: How you evaluate which solutions will work

She uses the Wells Fargo scandal as a cautionary tale. Wells Fargo set an outcome (increase accounts per customer) but didn't pair it with guardrail metrics or customer-centric framing. The result was fraud. The lesson: outcomes must be pursued by creating customer value, not at the expense of it.

Key arguments about the OST:

- It resolves the tension between business needs and customer needs by connecting them visually
- It builds shared understanding across the trio
- It helps teams adopt a continuous mindset by breaking large opportunities into smaller ones
- It unlocks better decision-making by forcing "compare and contrast" decisions instead of "whether or not" decisions
- It unlocks faster learning cycles because the problem space and solution space evolve together
- It builds confidence in knowing what to do next
- It simplifies stakeholder management by showing your work

Torres draws on Chip and Dan Heath's four villains of decision-making (from Decisive): looking too narrowly at problems, confirmation bias, short-term emotions, and overconfidence. The OST is designed to counteract all four.

She also introduces the concept of "whether or not" decisions vs. "compare and contrast" decisions. "Should we build this feature?" is a bad decision frame. "Which of these three opportunities should we pursue?" is a good one. Compare-and-contrast decisions reduce confirmation bias and lead to better outcomes.

## Part 2: The Continuous Discovery Habits

### Chapter 3: Focusing on Outcomes Over Outputs

The core argument: when teams are managed by outcomes rather than outputs, they get the autonomy to find the best solution. A fixed roadmap communicates false certainty. An outcome communicates productive uncertainty.

Torres distinguishes three types of outcomes:

- Business outcomes: Financial metrics or strategic initiatives (grow revenue, reduce costs, increase market share). Often lagging indicators.
- Product outcomes: How well the product moves the business forward. These are what product trios should own. They are leading indicators of business outcomes.
- Traction metrics: Usage of a specific feature or workflow. Useful but narrow.

The ideal setup: leadership assigns a product outcome, the trio discovers how to drive it. The product outcome should be a leading indicator of a business outcome.

Key lessons from this chapter:

- Teams should negotiate outcomes with leadership, not just receive them. It should be a two-way conversation.
- When facing a new outcome for the first time, start with a learning goal ("discover the opportunities that will drive engagement") before committing to a performance goal ("increase engagement by 10%"). Research shows that for complex tasks, learning goals outperform specific challenging goals until appropriate strategies are identified.
- S.M.A.R.T. goals have a role but are not one-size-fits-all. They work best when teams already have experience with a metric.
- Teams should pursue one outcome at a time, not multiple. Spreading thin leads to incremental progress on many things and big impact on nothing.
- Stick with an outcome for multiple quarters. The second and third quarters produce outsized results because you've already done the learning.

Anti-patterns to avoid:

- Pursuing too many outcomes at once
- Ping-ponging from one outcome to another quarter to quarter
- Setting individual outcomes instead of product-trio outcomes
- Choosing an output disguised as an outcome ("Launch an Android app" vs. "Increase mobile engagement")
- Focusing on one outcome to the detriment of all else (the Wells Fargo problem). Always pair with guardrail/health metrics.

### Chapter 4: Visualizing What You Know (Experience Mapping)

Before you can discover opportunities, you need to take inventory of what you already know. Torres recommends starting with an experience map of the customer's current experience.

The process:

1. Each trio member independently maps out what they think the customer's experience looks like today
2. The trio explores each other's perspectives (each member brings unique knowledge)
3. They merge perspectives into a shared experience map

The map is not truth. It contains hunches and possibilities. Its purpose is to make explicit what the team thinks they know, where they have open questions, and what they need to vet in upcoming interviews.

Key principles:

- Map the customer's experience, not your product's features
- Use the map to guide interview questions
- The map should evolve continuously as you learn more
- It gives structure to the opportunity space by identifying distinct moments in the customer journey

### Chapter 5: Continuous Interviewing

This is one of the longest and most important chapters. Torres's central claim: you cannot ask customers what they want and expect reliable answers. Decades of research on cognitive bias shows that people are bad at understanding their own behavior.

The core problem with direct questions:

- People describe their ideal behavior, not their actual behavior (the jeans example: "Fit is my number-one factor" but actually bought on Amazon based on brand and price)
- Our brains fabricate coherent but inaccurate explanations for our behavior (Gazzaniga's split-brain research on the "left brain interpreter")
- We overestimate how often we do things, speculate about what we did, and give identity-driven answers rather than behavior-driven ones

Torres tells her own cautionary tale: she built a passive-candidate recruiting tool because every recruiter said they wanted to source passive candidates. It flopped because recruiters actually sourced active candidates (they were measured on speed-to-fill). The product was built on what customers aspired to do, not what they actually did.

The solution: story-based interviewing. Instead of asking direct questions, ask customers to share specific stories about recent experiences.

Key distinctions:

- Research questions (what you want to learn) are different from interview questions (what you actually ask)
- Never ask about future behavior ("Would you use this?"). It's unreliable.
- Ask about specific past instances ("Tell me about the last time you...")
- Let the customer set the direction. What matters most to them trumps what you need to learn.

The interview structure Torres recommends:

1. Start broad: "Tell me about the last time you [did the thing related to your outcome]"
2. Let the customer's story unfold naturally
3. Dig into the details of their story (who, what, when, where, why, how)
4. Look for needs, pain points, and desires embedded in their story
5. Synthesize what you heard into an "interview snapshot"

Automating recruitment: Torres advocates building recruitment into the product itself (e.g., a post-interaction prompt asking "Got a few minutes to chat with our team?"). This creates a continuous pipeline of interview participants without the overhead of manual recruitment.

Interview snapshots: After each interview, create a one-page summary capturing the key opportunities (needs, pain points, desires) and any notable quotes or insights. These accumulate over time and feed the opportunity solution tree.

Anti-patterns:

- Asking leading questions
- Asking about future behavior
- Only interviewing when you have a specific question (you should always be interviewing)
- Treating interviews as validation rather than discovery
- Not drawing the customer's story to identify key moments

### Chapter 6: Mapping the Opportunity Space

Once you've been interviewing regularly, you'll accumulate a list of customer needs, pain points, and desires. The challenge: how do you make sense of them? A flat list doesn't work because opportunities have different sizes, overlap with each other, and are hard to compare.

Torres's solution: use a tree structure. The opportunity space should be organized as a hierarchy with parent-child and sibling relationships.

Key concepts:

- Parent-child relationships represent subsets. "I can't find anything to watch" is a parent. "I'm out of episodes of my favorite show" is a child (one reason among many).
- Sibling relationships represent distinct opportunities at the same level. Siblings should be similar (subsets of the same parent) but distinct (you can address one without addressing another).
- Top-level branches should represent distinct moments in time from the customer's experience map. This prevents overlap.

The power of trees:

- They let you deconstruct large, intractable problems into a series of smaller, more solvable problems
- They enable iterative delivery: solve one small opportunity completely, ship it, move to the next
- They make prioritization tractable: you only need to compare siblings, not the entire space

Building the tree:

1. Use your experience map to identify distinct top-level branches (moments in time)
2. Take inventory of opportunities from your interview snapshots
3. For each opportunity, ask: Is it framed as a need/pain point/desire (not a solution)? Have we seen it in more than one interview? Would addressing it drive our outcome?
4. Group opportunities under the appropriate branch
5. Add structure within each branch: group similar opportunities, identify parent-child relationships, ensure siblings are distinct

The tree is never done. It evolves continuously as you learn more.

Anti-patterns:

- Opportunities framed from the company's perspective instead of the customer's
- Vertical opportunities (parent with one child with one child) - indicates missing siblings or duplicate framing
- Opportunities with multiple parents - means they're framed too broadly
- Opportunities that aren't specific enough ("I wish this was easy to use")
- Opportunities that are solutions in disguise ("I wish I could fast-forward through commercials" - the real opportunity is "I don't like commercials")
- Capturing feelings as opportunities ("I'm frustrated") - feelings are signposts pointing to the real opportunity (the cause of the frustration)

### Chapter 7: Prioritizing Opportunities, Not Solutions

Torres's argument: product strategy doesn't happen in the solution space. It happens in the opportunity space. Strategy emerges from which outcomes you pursue, which customers you serve, and which opportunities you address.

Key principles:

- Focus on one target opportunity at a time. Spreading across many opportunities puts you back in waterfall mode.
- Use the tree structure to optimize prioritization. Start by comparing top-level siblings. Once you pick a winner, ignore the other branches. Compare children within that branch. Keep going until you reach a leaf node.
- Always choose a leaf-node opportunity (one with no children) as your target. This ensures you can deliver iterative value.

Four lenses for assessing opportunities:

1. Opportunity sizing: How many customers are affected? How often? (Rough estimates, not precise measurement)
2. Market factors: Table stakes vs. differentiators? External trends? Competitive positioning?
3. Company factors: Does it align with company vision, mission, strategy? Political capital required? Team strengths?
4. Customer factors: How important is this to customers? How satisfied are they with existing solutions?

Critical framing:

- These are relative judgments, not absolute scores. Compare siblings against each other.
- Do NOT mathematize this into a scoring formula. It's a subjective, messy decision and should stay that way. Quantifying it creates false precision and stops critical thinking.
- Choosing a target opportunity is a two-way door decision (reversible). You're committing to explore it further, not committing to build something. If you learn it's wrong, you course-correct.
- Research shows that when people view decisions as reversible, they continue to critically evaluate them. When they view decisions as irreversible, they only see positives of their choice and negatives of alternatives (confirmation bias).

Anti-patterns:

- Delaying decisions waiting for more data (analysis paralysis)
- Over-relying on one set of factors
- Working backwards from an expected conclusion
- Treating opportunity prioritization like feature prioritization

### Chapter 8: Supercharged Ideation

Torres transitions from "Discovering Opportunities" to "Discovering Solutions." The first habit: generating ideas effectively.

Core argument: your first idea is rarely your best idea. Creativity research shows that fluency (number of ideas) correlates with flexibility (diversity) and originality (novelty). The most original ideas tend to come toward the end of ideation sessions, not the beginning.

The problem with brainstorming: decades of research shows that individuals generating ideas alone outperform brainstorming groups. Groups produce fewer ideas, less diverse ideas, and less original ideas. Four reasons:

1. Social loafing (people work harder alone)
2. Group conformity (early ideas set the tone, people self-censor)
3. Production blocking (you forget your idea while someone else is talking)
4. Downward norm setting (group performance drops to the lowest performer)

Additionally, brainstorming groups suffer from "the illusion of group productivity" - they overestimate their performance and report high satisfaction despite lesser output.

The solution: alternate between individual ideation and group sharing. Research shows this captures the benefits of both approaches. Individuals generate ideas alone, share with the group, then go back to ideating alone (now inspired by others' ideas).

Torres's recommended process:

1. Review your target opportunity (make sure everyone understands it)
2. Generate ideas alone
3. Share ideas across the team
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until you have 15-20 ideas
5. Evaluate: remove ideas that don't address the target opportunity
6. Dot-vote as a team to select the top 3 (not 1) ideas to move forward with

Why three ideas, not one: you need a diverse set to enable compare-and-contrast decisions. You'll use assumption testing (next chapters) to whittle from three to one.

Getting unstuck techniques:

- Take frequent breaks, spread ideation throughout the day
- Take advantage of incubation (sleep on it)
- Look to analogous products for inspiration (not just competitors, but unrelated industries solving similar problems)
- Consider extreme users (power users, first-time users, people with disabilities)
- Allow wild ideas (they improve feasible ideas through mixing and matching)

Anti-patterns:

- Not including diverse perspectives
- Generating too many variations of the same idea instead of categorically different ones
- Limiting ideation to one session
- Selecting ideas that don't address the target opportunity

### Chapter 9: Identifying Hidden Assumptions

Torres's argument: the biggest risk in product development is not that you'll build the wrong thing. It's that you'll build something based on assumptions you didn't know you were making.

Five categories of assumptions:

1. Desirability: Do customers want this? Will they choose it?
2. Usability: Can customers figure out how to use it?
3. Feasibility: Can we build it? Do we have the technology and data?
4. Viability: Will it work for the business? Can we afford it? Does it scale?
5. Ethical: Could this cause harm? Are there data privacy concerns? Equity issues?

Methods for surfacing assumptions:

**Story mapping:** Walk through the step-by-step experience of how a customer would use your solution. For each step, generate assumptions across all five categories. A simple 5-step story map can yield 20+ assumptions.

**Pre-mortems (from Gary Klein):** Imagine it's six months in the future. Your product launched and was a complete failure. What went wrong? The key: phrase it as if the outcome is certain (it DID fail, not it MIGHT fail). Research shows this phrasing is critical for the technique to work.

**Walking the lines of your OST:** Work backwards from your solution to your outcome. "This solution will address the target opportunity because..." "Addressing the target opportunity will drive the desired outcome because..." Each "because" contains testable assumptions.

**Exploring potential harm:** Ask "What's the potential harm in offering this solution?" Questions to consider:
- What data are you collecting? Do customers understand how you'll use it?
- Does the product have addictive potential?
- Who is being left out?
- Does this contribute to or mitigate societal inequalities?
- How might trolls abuse this?
- The "front page test": if a journalist published your internal conversations about how this works, would that be a good thing?

Prioritizing assumptions (David Bland's assumption mapping):

Map each assumption on two dimensions:
- X-axis: How much evidence do we have? (left = strong evidence, right = weak evidence)
- Y-axis: How important is this to the idea's success? (top = critical, bottom = less critical)

Start testing the assumptions in the upper-right corner (most important, least evidence). These are your "leap of faith" assumptions. Go fast (10 minutes per idea max). The exercise doesn't need to be precise - you're mapping assumptions relative to each other.

Anti-patterns:

- Not generating enough assumptions (aim for 20-30 per idea)
- Phrasing assumptions negatively (phrase them as what needs to be TRUE for the idea to work)
- Most teams have blind spots in one category (usually ethical or viability)

### Chapter 10: Testing Assumptions, Not Ideas

This is the chapter that operationalizes the "15-20 discovery iterations per week" that Marty Cagan advocates for. The secret: test individual assumptions, not whole ideas. Testing a whole idea requires building a prototype and running a full usability study. Testing a single assumption can be done in a day.

The framework for each assumption test:

1. Identify the assumption to test
2. Design a simulation that will generate the behavior you need to observe
3. Define evaluation criteria upfront (how many people, how many need to exhibit the desired behavior)

Critical principle: define success criteria BEFORE running the test. This guards against confirmation bias and ensures the team agrees on what results mean.

On sample sizes and statistical significance:

Torres explicitly rejects the need for large-scale quantitative experiments in early discovery. Her argument: product teams are not scientists seeking truth. They are practitioners trying to mitigate risk. The goal is not to validate or invalidate anything. It's to gather confirming or disconfirming evidence.

She recommends testing with small numbers (as few as 5-10 people) and using the results as signals, not proof. False negatives and false positives will occur, but their cost is low when tests are small and fast. You triangulate across multiple small tests rather than relying on one large one.

Two essential tools every product team needs:

1. Unmoderated user testing: Post a stimulus and tasks, participants complete them on their own time, you get videos back the next day. What used to take weeks can be done in 1-2 days.
2. One-question surveys: Test specific assumptions with a single well-crafted question. Follow the same rules (ask about past behavior, not future intentions).

Other testing methods: data mining (look at existing behavioral data), prototype tests (in-person or unmoderated), A/B tests (later stages).

Anti-patterns:

- Overly complex simulations (keep tests to 1-2 days, a week at most)
- Using percentages instead of specific numbers for evaluation criteria ("70%" is ambiguous; "7 out of 10" is clear)
- Not defining enough evaluation criteria
- Testing with the wrong audience
- Designing for less than the best-case scenario (test with the most likely audience first; if it fails there, the signal is clearer)

### Chapter 11: Measuring Impact

Torres tells her own story from AfterCollege to illustrate the full cycle from discovery insight to measurable impact. Her team discovered that college students couldn't answer "What type of job do you want?" or "Where do you want to work?" They built a prototype that asked easier questions (school, major, graduation date) and recommended jobs based on proprietary data. They started with a crude approximation (manually crafted saved searches per major) and diverted a small percentage of traffic to test it.

Key lessons:

- Start small. Divert a small percentage of traffic. Use crude approximations before investing in sophisticated solutions.
- Instrument your product to measure the connection between your product changes and your product outcome.
- Don't stop at product outcomes. Test the connection between your product outcome and your business outcome. That connection is a theory, not a fact.
- Build data infrastructure iteratively, not as a massive upfront project.

Torres introduces the concept of "walking the lines of your OST" for measurement: trace the path from your assumption test results, to your solution's impact on the target opportunity, to the opportunity's impact on your product outcome, to the product outcome's impact on your business outcome. Each connection is a hypothesis you should verify.

Anti-patterns:

- Getting stuck trying to measure everything (start with what you need this week)
- Hyperfocusing on assumption tests and forgetting to check if you're driving your outcome
- Forgetting to test the connection between product outcome and business outcome

### Chapter 12: Managing the Cycles

Torres's key point: the process described in this book is not linear. It's messy. Teams will constantly loop back to earlier steps as they learn surprising things. This is normal and should be celebrated.

Four real-world stories illustrate different types of course-corrections:

1. Simply Business: Discovered through assumption testing that customers didn't want help with a problem (late payments) even though they complained about it in every interview. Killed the opportunity in a week, chose a new one the next day, and used already-scheduled interviews to learn about it. The fruit of discovery is often the time you save when you decide NOT to build something.

2. CarMax: Tackled an opportunity ("I want to feel confident this car is in good condition") but discovered through testing that the full solution wasn't feasible right now. Used a "now, next, future" framework to ship what they could immediately while planning the rest for later.

3. Farm Credit Services of America: Uncovered a key value proposition during discovery that could have been destroyed by their solution. Used discovery work to find a way to drive the outcome while preserving the value prop.

4. Snagajob: Had the luxury of embedding with a customer. Used that opportunity to solve one problem after another, each solution opening the door to the next. Over time, had massive impact on their outcome through a series of small wins.

The meta-lesson: no matter how good you get at discovery, you'll still encounter surprises. The habits give you the tools to respond to those surprises quickly and cheaply.

Anti-patterns:

- Expecting a linear process
- Giving up before small changes have time to add up
- Not celebrating course-corrections (they save you from building the wrong thing)

### Chapter 13: Show Your Work

Torres's argument: it's not enough to do good discovery if you aren't bringing stakeholders along. The most common failure mode is presenting conclusions (roadmaps, backlogs, release plans) and inviting stakeholders to an opinion battle you can't win (the HiPPO always wins in opinion battles).

The alternative: show your work using the same visual artifacts you use for discovery. Walk stakeholders through your thinking step by step:

1. Start with the outcome. Remind them what you're pursuing. Ask if anything has changed.
2. Share the opportunity space. Highlight top-level opportunities. Ask if they see anything missing.
3. Share how you prioritized. Walk through each decision point. Ask if they'd have decided differently.
4. Share your target opportunity in depth. Help them understand the customer need. Use interview snapshots.
5. Share solutions you generated. Ask if they have ideas. Be willing to swap in their ideas if they add diversity.
6. Share assumption maps and test results. Ask them to add assumptions you might have missed.

When you show your work, you're inviting stakeholders to co-create with you. Instead of defending conclusions, you're leveraging their expertise and improving your process. This leads to much more buy-in.

Tailor the detail to the stakeholder. Your boss might want weekly updates. Your CEO might need a 2-minute narrative once a month. But even with a busy CEO, still show the work (outcome, top opportunities, why you chose yours, top solutions, key test results).

Anti-patterns:

- Telling instead of showing (the curse of knowledge - your conclusions feel obvious to you but aren't to them)
- Overwhelming stakeholders with all the messy details
- Arguing with stakeholders about why their ideas won't work (instead, add their ideas to your assumption testing backlog and let the data decide)
- Sharing your work only at the end instead of continuously

## Part 3: Developing Your Continuous Discovery Habits

### Chapter 14: Start Small, and Iterate

Torres's practical advice for teams that want to adopt these habits:

- You don't need organizational permission to start. Find one customer to talk to. Form a trio even if it's informal.
- Work backward from assigned features to uncover implied opportunities and outcomes. Even if you're told what to build, you can still ask "What problem does this solve?" and "What outcome does this drive?"
- Use retrospectives to improve your discovery process. Ask: "What did we learn this sprint that surprised us?" and "How could we have learned that sooner?"
- Start with what's within your control. Don't wait for the perfect organizational conditions.

Anti-patterns:

- "That will never work here" mentality (Torres has seen these habits work at companies of all sizes, in all industries)
- Being the annoying champion for the "right way" (there is no one right way; adapt the habits to your context)
- Waiting for permission instead of starting with what you can control
- Letting perfect be the enemy of good (if next week is better than last week, you're on the right track)

## Key Arguments and Lessons (Synthesized)

### On the Nature of Product Work

- Product work is fundamentally about solving ill-structured problems. There are no right answers, only better or worse ones.
- The problem space and solution space evolve together. You can't fully define the problem before exploring solutions, and exploring solutions teaches you more about the problem.
- Discovery doesn't prevent failure. It reduces the cost of failure by helping you fail faster and cheaper.
- Digital products are never done. They must continuously evolve as markets change, customer needs shift, and technology advances.

### On Understanding Customers

- You cannot ask customers what they want. They don't know. Their self-reported behavior is unreliable.
- The purpose of interviewing is not to ask what to build. It's to discover opportunities (needs, pain points, desires).
- Stories about specific recent instances are far more reliable than generalizations about behavior.
- What customers say they want (aspirational) and what they actually do (behavioral) are often completely different.
- Interview continuously (weekly minimum). If you only talk to customers monthly, you're making a month's worth of decisions without customer input.

### On Decision-Making

- Avoid "whether or not" decisions. Always frame as "compare and contrast" across multiple options.
- Most discovery decisions are two-way doors (reversible). Move fast, test, and course-correct.
- When people view decisions as reversible, they continue to critically evaluate them. When they view decisions as irreversible, confirmation bias takes over.
- Don't mathematize subjective decisions. Scoring formulas create false precision and stop critical thinking.
- Analysis paralysis is more costly than making a wrong but reversible decision.

### On Outcomes vs. Outputs

- A fixed roadmap communicates false certainty. An outcome communicates productive uncertainty.
- Product outcomes (leading indicators the team can influence) are more useful than business outcomes (lagging indicators).
- Start with learning goals before performance goals when facing a new outcome.
- One outcome at a time. Stick with it for multiple quarters. The learning compounds.
- Always pair outcomes with guardrail metrics to prevent perverse incentives.

### On Ideation and Solutions

- Your first idea is rarely your best. Push past obvious solutions to find diverse and original ones.
- Individuals ideating alone outperform brainstorming groups. But alternating between individual ideation and group sharing captures the best of both.
- Always work with a set of three solutions, not one. This enables compare-and-contrast evaluation.
- Ideas are cheap. Don't fall in love with them. The opportunity is what matters.

### On Testing and Evidence

- Test assumptions, not whole ideas. This is what unlocks 15-20 discovery iterations per week.
- Define success criteria before running tests. This guards against confirmation bias.
- Product teams are not scientists. The goal is not truth but risk mitigation.
- Small, fast tests with 5-10 people provide useful signals. Triangulate across multiple tests rather than relying on one large study.
- False negatives and false positives are cheap when tests are small. They become expensive only when you're building the full thing.

### On Organizational Dynamics

- Don't present conclusions to stakeholders. Show your work. Walk them through your thinking.
- Opinion battles with stakeholders are unwinnable. Reframe the conversation around evidence and customer needs.
- The fruit of discovery is often the time you save when you decide NOT to build something.
- You don't need permission to start. Talk to anyone who resembles your customers. Form a trio informally. Start small.

### On the Process Itself

- The process is not linear. Expect to loop back constantly as you learn surprising things.
- Course-corrections should be celebrated, not feared. They mean the system is working.
- No matter how good you get, surprises will still happen. The habits give you tools to respond quickly.
- Don't let perfect be the enemy of good. If next week is better than last week, you're on the right track.
- All the methods in this book are templates, not recipes. Adapt them to your context.

## Bottom Line

The book is a practitioner's manual, not a theoretical text. Its core message can be reduced to one sentence: talk to your customers every week, map what you learn into a structured opportunity space, generate and test solutions against specific assumptions, and show your work to stakeholders along the way. Do all of this continuously, not as a one-time phase. The habits compound over time. Teams that adopt them report increased confidence, faster learning, fewer wasted features, and better business outcomes.
